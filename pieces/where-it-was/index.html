Where It Was

A river can't stay straight, and the ground remembers every place it tried.

In a bend, the water at the bed is slowed by friction and feels less of the outward push than the water above it, so the flow turns over itself in a slow helix. Fast water is thrown at the outer bank, which erodes, and slow water drops sand on the inner bank. The effect peaks a little past each bend, so bends grow and walk downstream until a neck closes and a loop is left behind as an oxbow. Here the migration follows curvature weighted toward upstream (Howard and Knutson's simplification of Ikeda, Parker and Sawai's bend theory). The faint lines are every place the channel has been. One honest gap: with the banks let go entirely, a real river stops being a single thread and braids. In the flume, meanders held only once the banks were planted (Braudrick et al., 2009, with alfalfa sprouts). This river can't braid.
